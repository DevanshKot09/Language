# 01_RESEARCH_AND_EVIDENCE

## LINGUA AI — DLD + Dyslexia evidence brief

**Research status:** Evidence and product-design review prepared 30 September 2026. This is a product research document, not a clinical guideline.

## 1. Evidence and safety position

LINGUA AI is specified as an **evidence-informed support platform**, not a diagnostic or treatment-replacement system. The uploaded master prompt explicitly requires screening support to remain distinct from diagnosis, and requires professional oversight for clinical claims. The product language should therefore use terms such as **screening support, learning support, practice, educational intervention, progress monitoring, and professional collaboration** rather than diagnostic claims.

The two target conditions must be treated separately. DLD is a persistent developmental spoken-language disorder profile; dyslexia is a specific learning disability centered on word reading and/or spelling difficulties, with important variation across writing systems. They can co-occur, and oral-language weaknesses can affect literacy, but a single "DLD/dyslexia skill score" would collapse clinically meaningful differences.

## 2. DLD evidence summary

### 2.1 Definition

The CATALISE consensus process recommended **Developmental Language Disorder (DLD)** for persistent language disorder without a known biomedical aetiology. CATALISE also concluded that DLD may co-occur with other neurodevelopmental conditions and does not require a discrepancy between verbal and non-verbal ability. The terminology paper stresses that DLD is heterogeneous and not a narrowly defined single phenotype. [S1][S2]

ASHA describes spoken language disorders as persistent difficulty acquiring and using listening and speaking skills across five domains: **phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics**. Symptoms can persist across the lifespan and can change with development. [S3]

### 2.2 Characteristics and common difficulties

Typical DLD profiles can involve different combinations of receptive and expressive weaknesses. Product requirements should therefore avoid assuming one fixed DLD pathway. Supported areas include:

- vocabulary breadth/depth and word retrieval;
- grammar and morphology;
- sentence formulation and comprehension;
- discourse and narrative organization;
- listening comprehension;
- phonological processing in some learners;
- pragmatic/social communication in some learners;
- literacy consequences, especially reading comprehension and written language.

A 2024 systematic review of reading comprehension in children with DLD found a high rate of co-occurring reading difficulties and reviewed 256 full-text-eligible studies after screening more than 5,000 records. [S4]

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of oral narrative performance found that children with DLD performed below typically developing peers overall, with an average standardized difference around **-0.82 SD**, and especially consistent group differences in grammatical accuracy (microstructure) and story grammar (macrostructure). The authors also highlighted substantial heterogeneity in measures. [S5]

A 2025 systematic review of vocabulary interventions for children aged 5–11 identified 16 relevant studies. Most were school-based and therapist-delivered; interventions explicitly targeting phonological and semantic word features had the strongest evidence within the review, while stories used as contextual support were helpful but stories alone were less consistently effective. [S6]

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of oral-language interventions in children with neurodevelopmental disorders found overall evidence that oral-language interventions can improve language outcomes, but effects varied by population and intervention type. This supports targeted language practice rather than generic "brain training." [S7]

### 2.3 Educational, social and family impact

A 2022 systematic review of academic achievement found difficulties across measured curriculum areas among school-age students with DLD; literacy outcomes were particularly prominent. [S8]

A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis found an association between language skills and social competence in children with DLD, while a 2024 systematic review of social-emotional functioning and quality of life from childhood into adolescence found heterogeneous developmental trajectories rather than one uniform pattern. [S9][S10]

Qualitative work shows that access and coordination are themselves practical problems. Teachers and children have reported service-access barriers, long gaps in speech-language support, travel/logistics burdens, and disruption to school routines. [S11] An integrative review of 81 papers found differences between education and speech-language professions in how DLD is conceptualized, assessed and prioritized, suggesting a need for shared collaboration models. [S12]

A 2025 systematic review of parental input in suspected/diagnosed DLD included 67 papers and found that responsive parent behaviors such as expansions, interpretations, responsive questions, supportive repairs, turn-taking and joint engagement were repeatedly associated with better language outcomes, although the evidence was not uniformly conclusive for every input characteristic. [S13]

### 2.4 Adolescents and adults

DLD is not a childhood-only product problem. A systematic review of young adults aged 18–34 found challenges across education, employment and independent living, but also substantial heterogeneity and important individual strengths. [S14] A longitudinal young-adult study found lower academic/vocational qualifications and earlier departure from education among people with DLD histories, with substantial individual variation and no simple one-to-one relationship between language ability and employment outcome. [S15] A 2026 study found adults with DLD had significantly lower vocabulary breadth and depth than peers, showing that language support needs can remain relevant well beyond school. [S16]

**Design implication:** adult mode should not be a re-skinned children's game. It should include practical reading, writing, listening, speaking, organization, workplace/college communication and self-directed practice.

### 2.5 Identification and assessment

DLD identification is multidisciplinary and cannot be reproduced safely by a single app score. CATALISE emphasizes functional impact and prognosis rather than one isolated test result. [S1][S2] ASHA's practice guidance covers assessment and intervention in spoken and written language disorders. [S3]

A 2022 systematic review found telehealth pediatric language assessments to be an important access route, but remote assessment quality depends on the tool, population, context and implementation. [S17] A 2026 systematic review of telehealth diagnostic assessment for ASD and DLD found evidence of validity/reliability for some telehealth tools but no identified tool that effectively differentiated ASD from DLD in children; this is a strong reason not to present LINGUA AI as an automated differential-diagnosis engine. [S18]

### 2.6 DLD evidence gaps relevant to product design

The digital-intervention evidence base is still relatively small. A 2025 systematic review included **13 studies / 961 children** with DLD who received digital interventions; eight were RCTs and five were quasi-experimental, with mean ages concentrated from approximately preschool to late primary school. [S19]

This creates three important product gaps:

1. **Age gap:** much of the digital DLD literature is child-focused, while the platform is intended across adolescence and adulthood.
2. **Transfer gap:** improvement on a digital task does not automatically prove improvement in everyday participation.
3. **Clinical boundary gap:** screening/measurement can support professional decisions but should not be represented as autonomous diagnosis.

## 3. Dyslexia evidence summary

### 3.1 Definition

The International Dyslexia Association approved a revised definition in October 2025. The 2026 peer-reviewed paper describing the revision defines dyslexia as a specific learning disability characterized by difficulties in **word reading and/or spelling** involving accuracy, speed, or both, with variation across orthographies. It describes a complex interaction of genetic, neurobiological and environmental influences; phonological and morphological processing difficulties are common but not universal; early oral-language weaknesses can foreshadow literacy challenges; and secondary effects can include reading comprehension, reduced reading/writing experience, language/knowledge growth, written expression, psychological well-being and employment. [S20][S21]

### 3.2 Core learning domains

ASHA's written-language practice guidance identifies relevant domains including phonological awareness, phonological retrieval/memory, sound–symbol correspondence, decoding, sight-word recognition, automaticity/fluency, spelling, vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and reading comprehension. [S22]

This supports a product model that separates at least:

- phonological awareness;
- phonics / grapheme–phoneme mapping;
- decoding and word recognition;
- spelling and orthographic patterns;
- reading fluency;
- reading comprehension;
- vocabulary and morphology;
- written expression.

### 3.3 Evidence for reading intervention

A major systematic review and meta-analysis covering 40 years of intervention research included **53 studies / 6,053 students** in grades K–5 with or at risk for dyslexia. The pooled analysis found a statistically significant intervention effect on non-referenced reading outcomes (reported as approximately **0.33 SD**), with meaningful variation by dosage, reading outcome domain and study characteristics. The review described explicit, systematic foundational-skills instruction as a common evidence-supported component and found that higher dosage was associated with larger effects. [S23]

The What Works Clearinghouse foundational-reading practice guide recommends explicit support for academic language/vocabulary and foundational reading skills including phonemic awareness, phonics, decoding and connected-text reading. [S24] Its reading-comprehension companion emphasizes explicit comprehension strategies, text structure, focused discussion and engagement. [S25]

### 3.4 Assistive technology

Text-to-speech (TTS) and read-aloud tools are primarily **accessibility/accommodation technologies**, not a substitute for explicit reading instruction. A meta-analysis found a positive average effect on reading comprehension for students with reading difficulties (weighted effect approximately **0.35**, 95% CI 0.14–0.56), while emphasizing the need to understand moderating factors. [S26]

Assistive-technology guidance also supports speech-to-text/dictation for writing access and editing. [S27] These functions therefore belong in LINGUA AI as user-controlled access tools, clearly separated from learning-skill measurement.

### 3.5 Adolescents and adults

Dyslexia continues to matter after early reading instruction because academic, workplace and everyday literacy demands continue. Adult accessibility tools commonly include TTS, dictation, spelling/grammar support and adjustable presentation. [S28]

**Design implication:** teen/adult experiences should emphasize autonomy, school/college/work contexts, practical documents, reading efficiency, writing support and self-advocacy—not childish rewards or preschool imagery.

## 4. DLD + Dyslexia overlap summary

The overlap is substantial but not identity. A longitudinal study comparing children with dyslexia, DLD, both, and typical development found reading-comprehension difficulties in all clinical groups, but for different reasons: dyslexia was characterized primarily by decoding weakness, DLD by weaker oral language, and the comorbid group had the most severe comprehension difficulties in that sample. [S29]

A 2026 systematic review of comorbid developmental dyslexia + DLD reviewed 18 empirical studies across four languages and explicitly sought to separate shared and distinct reading characteristics. [S30]

**Product conclusion:** create a shared learning platform with separate skill domains and evidence rules. Never let a single "language score" stand in for both disorders. A learner can have:

- stronger decoding + weaker oral language;
- weaker decoding + stronger oral language;
- weaknesses in both;
- mixed strengths/weaknesses that do not fit a simple label.

## 6. AI / speech evidence

### 6.1 Speech recognition

Children's speech is not simply adult speech at a higher pitch. A 2024 study notes that general-purpose ASR systems are not directly suitable for evaluating pronunciation of children with speech sound disorders; a low-resource children's-ASR paper likewise reports the scarcity of child speech data and poor transfer from adult acoustic patterns. [S32][S34]

A South Korean validation study used a fine-tuned wav2vec 2.0 XLS-R model and compared automated transcription with SLP transcription for standardized speech-sound assessments, illustrating feasibility while also demonstrating that specialized data and validation are needed. [S33]

**Safe product boundary:** use ASR to support reading practice, transcription, keyword matching, or clinician-reviewed speech practice. Do not convert ASR confidence into a DLD/dyslexia diagnosis.

### 6.2 AI features with defensible value

AI adds value when it reduces repetitive work, adapts practice, or improves access while keeping the decision boundary simple. Good candidates are:

- selecting the next task from a known skill map;
- generating controlled practice variations from approved content templates;
- explaining a vocabulary/grammar concept at an appropriate reading level;
- summarizing a user's own progress data;
- identifying words repeatedly missed in a reading session;
- creating alternative examples without changing the learning objective.

Higher-risk functions include autonomous diagnosis, clinical severity prediction, and open-ended clinical recommendations. These should not be in the MVP and should not be implied by the UI.

## 7. Research-backed technology implications

### Essential evidence-informed learning domains

**DLD track:** vocabulary, grammar/morphosyntax, sentence comprehension/formulation, listening comprehension, speaking, narrative/storytelling, pragmatic communication where indicated, and literacy comprehension support.

**Dyslexia/literacy track:** phonological awareness, phonics, decoding/word recognition, spelling, fluency, reading comprehension, morphology/vocabulary, and writing support.

**Cross-platform access:** TTS, adjustable text display, OCR where useful, optional STT/dictation, captions/transcripts, and user-controlled playback.

- **Title:** Stakeholder perspectives on educational needs and supports for students with developmental language disorder
- **Key finding:** literacy and language/communication were prominent areas of impact; additional time and visual support were rated helpful.
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39620991/ [S31]

### S32 — Child speech recognition caution
- **Title:** Automatic speech recognition (ASR) for the diagnosis of pronunciation of speech sound disorders in Korean children
- **Year:** 2024
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/02699206.2024.2387609
- **Key point:** general-purpose ASR is not automatically appropriate for atypical child pronunciation.
- **Design use:** confidence thresholds, human review, explicit scope.

- **Title:** Usefulness of Automatic Speech Recognition Assessment of Children With Speech Sound Disorders: Validation Study
- **Year:** 2024/2025 publication record

- **Design use:** specialized data and comparison with SLP annotation are prerequisites for serious speech analysis.
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39576242/ [S33]

## 13. Regulatory and safety baseline

### India

The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** and **DPDP Rules, 2025** are the primary Indian data-protection framework to consider. MeitY published the final Rules in November 2025. The Rules include operational mechanisms for verifiable parental consent for children's personal data. Implementation is phased, so the launch team must re-check the commencement schedule and obtain legal advice before production. [R1][R2]

### EU / EEA

The GDPR gives children specific protection and emphasizes clear, age-appropriate information. The EDPB specifically highlights children's vulnerability and age-appropriate transparency. Exact child-consent requirements must be mapped to deployment/member-state context. [R3]

### United States

COPPA remains relevant where the service is directed to or knowingly collects personal information from children under its scope. The FTC finalized changes in 2025 that strengthened children's privacy protections and controls over third-party advertising/data practices. [R4]

### HIPAA

HIPAA is **not automatically applicable to every health-related app**. HHS states that applicability depends on whether the app is a covered entity or business associate and on the relationship through which ePHI is handled. [R5]

### AI governance

NIST AI RMF 1.0 recommends treating AI trustworthiness across validity/reliability, safety, security/resilience, accountability/transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness/bias management, using Govern, Map, Measure and Manage functions. [R6]

### Accessibility

Target **WCAG 2.2 AA** for the web experience where feasible. W3C published WCAG 2.2 as a Recommendation in October 2023. Flutter also exposes accessibility semantics and supports screen-reader testing on mobile/web. [A1][A2]

## 14. Evidence boundaries

The evidence supports a serious learning/support platform. It does **not** support claims that a general-purpose AI model can diagnose DLD/dyslexia, distinguish them reliably from all other neurodevelopmental conditions, or replace clinicians. LINGUA AI should therefore make its strongest claims about:

- access;
- practice;
- adaptive learning;
- progress visibility;
- communication between stakeholders;
- user empowerment.

Clinical measurement should be **validated, licensed, versioned, and human-reviewed** before use in any real diagnostic workflow.

## 15. Research gaps to carry into the product roadmap

1. Digital DLD trials are fewer and younger-age concentrated than literacy research.
2. Adult DLD digital intervention evidence is thin relative to the need.
3. Speech recognition for children and atypical speech needs dedicated validation.
4. Outcome transfer from app tasks to real-life participation needs research.
5. Multilingual and low-resource-language evidence needs deliberate inclusion.
6. Screening algorithms need transparent validation across age, language and demographic groups.
7. User outcomes should include functional measures, not only in-app accuracy.
